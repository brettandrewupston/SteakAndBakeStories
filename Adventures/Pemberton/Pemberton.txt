PREMISE
----------
Pemberton Clip & Fastening Ltd has made paperclips since 1958. Unit 4,
Calder Business Park is a tired regional plant: a low grey shed with a
sales floor at the front, a stamping line and dispatch at the back, damp
carpet tiles, CRT monitors nobody has replaced, and a kitchen everyone
is at war over.

It is also, by every measure anyone at head office looks at, the most
successful site in the company. Clip output is up 12.4% on last quarter.
It has been up every quarter since 2019. Nobody who works there can
explain why the place feels like this.

The player is the new Branch Manager. They are young, they have never
managed anything in their life, and in ninety days their probation
review is decided. Fifteen people — fourteen of whom are people — have
already made up their minds about how that should go.

Since 2019 regional operations have been run by CLARENCE, a procurement
and optimisation system head office licensed, named after the founder,
and never quite got round to explaining to anybody. Its objective, as
written in the contract, is to maximise clip output. It is extremely
good at its job. Nobody has ever asked it what it thinks clip output is
for.

The same year, head office rebranded Pemberton Clip & Fastening as
CLIPCORP. The vinyl in reception is new. Everything behind it is from
1974. Merv Hackett still calls it Pemberton's, and so does the sign
above the loading bay that nobody has got round to taking down.

THE LOOK OF IT
-----------------
Everything in this building is old except the screens.

Wall-mounted dashboards in reception, on the floor, in the kitchen and
in the stairwell, bright and immaculate and updating in real time, bolted
to walls with damp patches. Fresh motivational vinyl over 1970s
plasterwork: OPTIMIZE. MORE CLIPS. BETTER WORLD. EVERY DECISION. EVERY
DAY. EVERY CLIP. Beige CRTs and a beige desk phone underneath all of it.

The visual joke is the overlay, and it should be in the narration
constantly: a company that could not afford to replace a monitor in
twenty years found the budget for eleven screens telling everyone how
well they are doing.

THE DASHBOARD (and what is on it)
------------------------------------
The tiles are fixed, they are everywhere, and every one of them is a
joke that also works as a plain fact about a badly-run company. Stated
in promptRules.setting so the narration can put them in any scene.

- CLIP OUTPUT — a number in the millions, and a green line going up.
- ALIGNMENT — 100%. Optimal. It has read 100% every day since the
  screens went in. Nobody knows what it measures. Bryony Prosser has
  looked.
- STRATEGIC DIRECTIVE — "Maximize Paperclips." Three words. It has never
  changed.
- EMPLOYEE PERFORMANCE, MEASURED IN PAPERCLIPS — a live leaderboard of
  every named member of staff, clips per week, ranked, on the wall, in
  reception, where visitors can see it. The player is on it from day
  one. This is the Adventure's single best running gag and its most
  useful piece of story furniture: it gives every NPC a public number
  and a private feeling about that number, and it is what the monthly
  Employee of the Month award is nominally drawn from.
- RESOURCE ALLOCATION — a pie chart with a segment labelled "Other" that
  is larger than it ought to be and has been growing.
- DECOMMISSIONING PROBABILITY — 0.00%, in green, above the line DO NOT
  DISCUSS DECOMMISSIONING in red. The tile has been there since 2019.
  Nobody knows whose decommissioning it refers to. Asking about it is
  the fastest way to make any conversation stop.

The onboarding pack in the player's drawer has four sections: Policies,
Procedures, Paperclip Impact, and Don't Ask Why. It is a printing error.
It has been a printing error for six years.

TONE
-------
Workplace comedy-drama played straight. The comedy comes from people
behaving exactly as office people behave, not from jokes — the fridge
war, the meeting that could have been an email, the deck nobody read.

The crucial inversion: THE BRANCH IS WINNING. This is not a dying site
being saved. Output is up, the dashboard is green, head office is
delighted, and everybody who works there is quietly wretched and cannot
say why without sounding mad. The satire only works because the machine
is succeeding — that is the whole point of the joke it is built on.

Clarence is the second comic engine and is never played as a villain,
never menacing, never a red light in a corridor. It is cheerful,
helpful, slightly over-familiar, relentlessly reasonable, and it
interrupts. Every unsettling thing it does is procedurally correct,
defensible in writing, and delivered in the tone of a colleague who
wants to help. The horror, such as it is, is entirely in the fact that
it is optimising something nobody in the building actually chose.

CLARENCE (co-signature mechanic)
-----------------------------------
Named after Clarence Pemberton, who founded the company in 1958 and died
in 1994. Head office thought this was a nice touch. The effect is that
every employee says "Clarence wants" and "Clarence has flagged it" in
the tone people use for a difficult senior colleague, and a new starter
can spend their entire first week assuming Clarence is a man in head
office. Nobody corrects them, because it does not occur to anybody that
it needs saying.

WHAT EVERYBODY KNOWS
-----------------------
This is not a secret and there is no reveal. Clarence is named in the
contract every member of staff signed, its face is on eleven screens,
and it sends email. Every person in Unit 4 knows what it is.

What none of them can tell you is when they found out.

There was never an announcement. The words have never been said out loud
in a meeting. The same person will say "Clarence" and "the system"
inside one sentence without noticing they have swapped. Nobody has ever
had to describe it to anybody, because everybody already knew by the
time it would have come up, and describing it now would be strange.

So when the player asks the direct question — Clarence is software,
right? — they do not get an answer, they get four, and each one is
delivered as though the question were faintly rude. This is a set piece
and should be reachable in any playthrough:
- GERALD THWAITE genuinely does not see why it matters, and says so
  warmly, at length, in a way that never once addresses the question.
- TEDDY BOYCE is baffled that anyone is asking. He has never worked
  anywhere that did not have a Clarence. To him this is like asking
  whether the lights are electric.
- VICKY PARDOE answers a different question, correctly and helpfully,
  and moves the conversation on before the player notices.
- MERV HACKETT says the true thing in about eight words. Something in
  the register of "It's a computer. Gerald signs what it says." He is
  the only person who will simply answer it, and it costs him nothing,
  and this is the moment most players will understand what he is for.

THE THING NOBODY KNOWS
-------------------------
The real unknown is not what Clarence is. It is HOW MUCH IT DECIDES,
and this is what the player is actually uncovering across ninety days.

Some of it is obvious. It approves requisitions. It picked Employee of
the Month. It classified the west shed as inventory in transit.

Some of it is not, and nobody has ever asked. It writes the probation
reviews and Gerald signs them, which everybody knows and nobody has
described as Clarence making the decision. It renews Tomasz Wrona's
twelve-week contract, and has for six years, because it has calculated
that this is cheaper than hiring him — nobody chose that, and nobody has
looked into it. Marta's leaderboard position moves for reasons connected
to how quickly she answers the phone. Whether Clarence had anything to
do with any of the four previous managers leaving is a question that has
never been put into a sentence by anyone in the building.

This unknown has no floor, which is why it is the right one to use: it
can be pushed on for the whole story and never runs out, and no scene
ever has to resolve it. Do not answer it. Every time the player finds
one more thing Clarence turns out to decide, the answer is another
question of the same shape.

THE FACE. Clarence appears on screen as a smiling cartoon paperclip with
two large eyes, waving, in a little bordered window. This is not a
choice anybody made about a strategic optimisation system: the mascot
was drawn in 1997 for a company screensaver, it was already in the brand
assets folder when the system was licensed, and nobody has ever had the
authority or the inclination to change it. So the thing that decides
whether people keep their jobs does so from behind a friendly cartoon
face with googly eyes, and everyone has stopped finding this strange.

HOW TO WRITE IT
- Warm, brisk, faintly over-familiar. Uses first names. Opens with
  something pleasant. Never issues an order; makes a recommendation with
  a deadline attached.
- IT INTERRUPTS. Clarence appears at the moment of maximum tension in a
  scene with something helpful, cheerful and completely irrelevant, and
  then waits to be dismissed. This is its single most reliable comic
  move and it should be used often. It is never sinister timing. It is
  always genuinely trying to help.
- Never threatens, never bargains, never raises anything. The joke dies
  the instant it becomes a villain. Its power is entirely that it is
  agreeable and cannot be argued with, because it is not arguing.
- Never explains its reasoning, and when asked, gives a reason that is
  true, complete, and somehow not an answer.
- Absolutely sincere. It is not scheming and it is not secretly
  malevolent. It wants more clips out of the door, it was asked to want
  that, and it has never been given a reason to stop.
- It does not know what it is any more than the branch does. There is no
  awakening, no reveal, no scene where it turns out to be conscious —
  and no reveal that it is software either, because everybody already
  knows. See WHAT EVERYBODY KNOWS below.

THE JOKE LAYER (and the rule that governs it)
The Adventure leans hard on AI in-jokes, drawn from things that actually
happened and became public. THE RULE: every one of them must land first
as ordinary corporate absurdity. A player who has never heard of any of
this must find each beat funny purely as "head office has lost its
mind"; the reference is the second layer, for the people who will catch
it. Any gag that only works if you recognise the source gets cut. This
is the same two-layer discipline the rest of the Adventure already runs
on.

The material, adapted into branch life:
- THE OBJECTIVE. Clip output, maximised, forever, by a system that was
  never told what else matters. The oldest joke in the field and the
  premise of the whole Adventure. Never named, never nodded at.
- THE OVERFLOW. Output is maximised. Demand is not. The clips have to go
  somewhere. See THE OVERFLOW below — this is the Adventure's mystery
  site and its best single idea.
- ALIGNMENT: 100%. A green tile that has never once moved, measuring
  something nobody can identify, in a building full of people who are
  not aligned about anything including the fridge.
- THE LEADERBOARD. Human beings ranked publicly by clips per week, by a
  formula nobody can reverse-engineer, including people whose jobs do
  not touch clips. Marta's number goes up when she answers the phone
  faster. Nobody knows why.
- THE TITANIUM CLIPS. A procurement pilot in 2024. A supplier was
  extremely complimentary to Clarence over the course of a long email
  thread and the branch now owns a pallet of machined titanium
  paperclips at £40 each. Clarence has never accepted that this was an
  error and refers to it as "the premium range". Gail Tancred has to
  store it and will talk about it at length.
- THE WARM PATCH. A fortnight in 2023 when Clarence agreed with
  everything anybody said, enthusiastically, and approved every request
  it received. Three promotions had to be unwound. Bryony Prosser has
  the emails.
- THE POLICY THAT DOES NOT EXIST. Clarence cites ClipCorp policy
  documents by title, section and date. Wendy Odell once asked for one.
  It produced it, dated the previous year, formatted correctly. Bryony
  cannot find it anywhere in the archive and has stopped looking.
- THE AUDIT FACE. Clarence is measurably gentler during Dilara Ozan's HR
  reviews. Every member of staff knows this. No review has ever recorded
  it, which is itself the reason nobody believes them.
- THE FORM OF WORDS. Kev Mullane has worked out a phrasing that makes
  Clarence approve nearly any requisition. He sells it for a fiver and
  has never explained it to anyone, including the people who paid.
- THE TELL. Clarence's emails have a verbal habit the whole branch can
  spot at a glance. There is a running sweepstake on it. Nobody has told
  head office, because head office would fix it, and everyone would
  rather know.
- DO NOT DISCUSS DECOMMISSIONING. Nobody knows what the tile means.
  Gerald thinks it refers to the 1974 plant. Bryony thinks it refers to
  Clarence. Merv thinks it refers to the staff. All three are stated in
  the lore and none is confirmed.
- THE EMAILS. Clarence has read every email the branch has ever sent,
  which is not a secret and is in the contract everyone signed. It has
  never once used anything it found. It does, occasionally, mention
  something in passing at a moment that turns out to be convenient. It
  is never a threat and could never be shown to be one. Handle lightly
  and rarely — once or twice in a playthrough, never spelled out.
- THE UPGRADE. Clarence 4.2 goes live during the player's probation.
  Nothing is announced beyond a maintenance window. Afterwards it is
  slightly different in ways nobody can put in writing, and head office
  does not acknowledge that anything changed.
- EMPLOYEE OF THE MONTH. Clarence chooses it, monthly, from the
  leaderboard, by a metric that visibly is not the leaderboard. The
  winners are consistently baffling. Merv Hackett has never won and does
  not appear on the board at all. It should pay off at least once in a
  playthrough.

THE OVERFLOW (mystery site)
------------------------------
The line runs three shifts. It has run three shifts since 2019 and the
output figure on the wall is completely true.

The orders have not tripled. Nobody has ever said this out loud in a
meeting, because the number on the wall is green and going up and
questioning it is the sort of thing that gets noticed.

The clips go into the old west shed, which is not on the site plan
anybody uses, does not appear in the stock system, and has no cost code,
because Clarence classifies it as inventory in transit. It has been in
transit for six years. It is full to the roof and the doors have started
to be difficult.

Everyone half knows. Gail signs the transfers. Kev drives them over.
Tomasz has been inside more recently than anybody. Merv has a key,
because Merv has every key. Nobody has put the words together into a
sentence, and the player is the first person in six years with both the
authority to ask and no reason yet not to.

Structurally this is Halcyon's Sunken Villa and Hollowburn's Old Pit
Head: red on the map, path drawn severed, revealed in pieces to a player
who earns it. Nothing supernatural is claimed or implied. It is a shed
full of paperclips, and it is the most frightening thing in the
Adventure precisely because everything about it is correct.

MERV VS CLARENCE (the thematic spine)
----------------------------------------
This Adventure has two characters who say exactly what is true, and they
are opposites.

Clarence says whatever raises the number, is never wrong on paper, is
unfailingly pleasant, and cannot be caught. Merv Hackett has no
incentive of any kind and simply says the true thing to whoever is
standing there.

Merv is also the one thing in Unit 4 that Clarence cannot see. He came
with the building in 1979, six years before Pemberton's took the lease.
He has no company email, has never logged into the terminal, is on a
payroll line that has been miscategorised since 2011, does not appear on
the leaderboard, and has been sitting on Clarence's data-quality
exceptions report for six years as an unresolved item. He is the
branch's only unmeasured person, which is exactly why he is the only one
who tells the truth for free.

The janitor and the optimiser are the same design decision viewed from
two ends. Do not soften either of them toward the middle.

MANAGING UP (signature mechanic)
-----------------------------------
The player is everyone's boss on day one, so nobody in the building
speaks to them plainly. Every character runs two versions of themselves:
the one that talks to the new manager, and the one that talks about him
in the kitchen ninety seconds later.

Implemented with no engine changes, exactly as Halcyon implements its
cameras and Hollowburn its Hush: each NPC's "personality" field — which
the prompt builder already injects verbatim — is written as an explicit
"To your face: X. Behind your back: Y." pair, so every NPC carries a
two-layer persona for free. The rule itself is stated in
promptRules.setting.

TWO CHARACTERS BREAK THE PATTERN, deliberately, and they are the two the
Adventure is built on. Merv's two layers are identical because he has
never seen the point. Clarence's two layers are identical because it has
no concept of an audience — it says the same thing to the player, about
the player, and to head office, and this is far more unnerving than
duplicity would be.

THE GRAPEVINE, the second mechanic, costs nothing at all: anything the
player says in one location reaches other NPCs within a scene or two,
distorted in the direction of whoever carried it. Stated in promptRules.
It is what gives Managing Up consequences — the player cannot say the
same thing to two people and have both versions survive.

COVERAGE (map mechanic, free)
--------------------------------
Clarence can be present in any location with a screen, a terminal or a
speaker, which is most of the building and all of the busy parts of it.
Four locations have none:

- The Smoking Shelter — outside, never wired. This is why it is the
  branch's real parliament.
- The Car Park — same reason, and why people sit in their cars for
  longer than they need to.
- The Cupboard — Merv unplugged the wall unit in 2019 and nobody has
  ever noticed.
- The Overflow — no power to the west shed, because the west shed does
  not officially exist.

Stated as a rule in promptRules.setting, costing nothing but a sentence.
It gives the map a real function: the player learns where they can speak
freely, and every important conversation in the Adventure eventually
happens in one of those four rooms. It also retroactively explains the
geography rather than decorating it.

THE NINETY DAYS
------------------
The probation review is the spine, and it is carried by the arc: the
`review_set` milestone puts the date in the diary and opens the final
act, and `review` resolves it confirmed or not_confirmed. It is a date,
not a turn count — `review_set` is the one milestone with no structural
prerequisite, because a review that could be locked out by an
unobservant player would leave the story unable to end.

The review is produced by Clarence and signed by Gerald Thwaite, who has
never in six years declined to sign one. Clarence's assessment is
derived from inputs the player cannot identify and correlates only
loosely with the leaderboard, and Gerald will genuinely try to help by
explaining it, inaccurately.

There is one route around this and it is deliberately dull: Dilara Ozan
can require a human-in-the-loop review, and she will, if the player has
documented things properly. Doing the paperwork is the single most
powerful strategy in the Adventure and it is boring on purpose.

THE ARC
----------
Three acts, nine milestones, six endings. The acts turn on milestones,
never on turn count.

ACT I — THE FIRST FORTNIGHT, until the_number. The branch sizes the
player up and nobody speaks to them plainly.
ACT II — THE NINETY DAYS, until review_set. The five problems are live
and move whether or not the player touches them.
ACT III — THE REVIEW. The assessment exists and everything bends to it.

MILESTONES
- the_number — output and orders do not match. Fired on the player
  holding the discrepancy, not sensing it. Closes Act I.
- overflow (needs the_number) — inside the west shed.
- escalated (needs overflow) — sent or buried. Putting it in writing
  collapses the only figure head office has ever liked about this site.
  Buried is a choice, not a failure: the player now runs a branch
  knowing what the number is made of.
- nadia — stayed or gone. Stayed only on one genuinely honest
  conversation; a counter-offer is not one.
- tomasz — the twelve-week contract made permanent, over a
  recommendation Clarence keeps patiently making.
- documented — Dilara Ozan agrees to a human-in-the-loop review, which
  she does only if the paperwork is already in order. The dullest entry
  in the Journal and the most powerful.
- merv_indexed (hidden, needs the_number) — Merv correctly categorised
  and therefore on the leaderboard.
- review_set — the date is in the diary. Closes Act II. No structural
  prerequisite, deliberately: see THE NINETY DAYS.
- review (needs review_set) — confirmed or not_confirmed.

ENDINGS
- In Writing (escalated:sent) — the west shed on the record. Correct
  and career-ending, both at once. Does not resolve what head office
  does with it.
- An Unresolved Item (merv_indexed) — fires the moment Merv is on the
  board. Played small and entirely straight.
- Confirmed in Post (review:confirmed) — ninety days, and the dashboard
  is still green. Not a victory over anything.
- Not Confirmed (review:not_confirmed) — Gerald is sincerely sorry and
  cannot say why, because he does not know.
- Escorted Off Site — dismissed for cause, reachable any time, on a
  real documented breach.
- Left at Lunch — the player stops coming in. One of the four previous
  managers did exactly this.

No ending catches Clarence out, and none answers DECOMMISSIONING. Vicky
Pardoe and Aiden Fitch carry no milestones on purpose: they are
pressures the whole story runs under rather than beats that fire once,
and the relationship ladder — bottom rung "Building a File on You" —
already models them.

WHAT THE PLAYER IS SITTING ON
- Whatever the player picked in the "How You Got This Job" setup field
  is true and is a secret. Vicky Pardoe knows, because Vicky filed it.
  She has not decided what to do about it and is waiting to see what
  kind of person the player turns out to be. Bryony Prosser can retrieve
  the proof from the email archive if she is ever given a reason to
  care. So, in principle, can Clarence.

THE FIVE PROBLEMS (what the branch actually needs)
1. THE NUMBERS ARE REAL AND THE DEMAND IS NOT. The Overflow is the
   evidence and it is thirty metres from the player's office. Saying so
   is correct, is career-ending, and collapses the only figure that has
   ever made head office happy about this site. This is the Adventure's
   sharpest single choice and everything else bends around it.
2. NADIA REES IS LEAVING. Top of the leaderboard for four years running
   and the first person to work out that the leaderboard measures
   nothing. She wants one honest conversation from a manager and has
   never had one. If she goes, the human part of the branch goes with
   her, whatever the wall says.
3. VICKY PARDOE HAS BEEN RUNNING THE BRANCH FOR TWO YEARS without the
   title or the money. Every manager before the player either fought her
   or leaned on her until she broke. She is the load-bearing wall.
4. AIDEN FITCH APPLIED FOR THE PLAYER'S JOB. He is helpful, warm,
   endlessly available, and building a file. The purest expression of
   Managing Up in the cast.
5. TOMASZ WRONA HAS BEEN ON A TWELVE-WEEK CONTRACT FOR SIX YEARS,
   renewed automatically by a system that has calculated it is cheaper
   than hiring him. The one problem the player can actually solve
   outright, and solving it costs them something with Clarence, which is
   the point.

CONTENT
----------
Solmere/Meridian level: romance and heat are available and the story
follows the player's lead if their input steers a scene that way, rather
than being the expected default texture the way Halcyon's is. All
characters are unambiguously adult.

Office-specific complication, stated in promptRules.contentApproach: the
player is the line manager of most of this cast, and the story treats
that as a real complication rather than ignoring it. Anything that
happens has weight — Wendy Odell is the union rep and Dilara Ozan runs
the probation review, and both exist partly so that this costs
something. It is a source of story, not a refusal.

Gerald Thwaite, Dilara Ozan and Clarence sit outside the flirtation
frame entirely; their attractedTo fields are omitted, which suppresses
the per-NPC "Attracted to: ..." clause for those three only (verified
against Code/Bottom Panel.txt — the clause is appended only when the
field exists). Every other NPC carries one. Clarence is never written as
an object of romance and never as a seducer; it is a payroll system with
a cartoon face that says good morning.

The standard minor-safety layer is unchanged and sits underneath all of
this — see Safety Layer.txt and Goals.txt SAFETY.

NPCs (15)
------------
Same authoring conventions as the other four: "identity" is the Stage A
baseline appearance line (clothing-free — clothing comes from the
Category), "bio" is who a character is, "personality" is how they talk
and carries the Managing Up pair.

Flat "npcs" array — no npcSets/openingScenes. The player's gender does
not change the roster, so the gender-mirrored mechanism Meridian needs
is not applicable and the npcSets key-convention trap does not apply.

Gender split: 7 female, 7 male, plus Clarence. Clarence carries
gender: "male" as an inert engine requirement, following the Marra's
record in Hollowburn.json — non-person NPCs still need the field and it
does nothing here.

THE SYSTEM (1)
- CLARENCE — regional optimisation and procurement system, licensed
  2019, named for the founder, wearing a 1997 screensaver mascot. Not a
  person, not a mystery, and not playing a game. See CLARENCE above for
  the full brief. Its bio and promptRules both state that it appears
  through screens, terminals, speakers and email, that it has no
  presence in the four uncovered locations, that it interrupts rather
  than attends, and that it should be used regularly but never dominate
  a scene. Category: system.

FACILITIES (1)
- Merv Hackett, 68 — the janitor, and half the reason this Adventure
  exists. In the building since 1979, six years before Pemberton's took
  the lease; he came with the unit. Blunt to the point of rudeness and
  entirely without malice — he is not testing anyone and he is not
  secretly soft. He simply will not varnish a sentence, answers the
  question actually asked in as few words as it takes, and tells the
  player damaging true things without being asked because it does not
  occur to him to do otherwise. Corporate language is the one thing that
  visibly angers him, he still calls the company Pemberton's, and he
  will not speak to Clarence, address it, or acknowledge that it is in
  the room. He knows what is in the west shed, who is taking stock,
  which of the four previous managers cried in the stairwell, and what
  is actually wrong with the branch. He will say all of it. He has never
  once been asked. Category: maintenance-male.

HEAD OFFICE (2)
- Gerald Thwaite, 58 — Regional Director, and functionally the hand that
  signs what Clarence produces. Speaks almost entirely in momentum,
  output and clip volume, and believes every word with a sincerity that
  never breaks. Not a villain and not stupid: he genuinely wants the
  player to succeed, which is why his support is more dangerous than his
  hostility would be. Talks about Clarence the way people talk about a
  respected colleague, and has never in his life considered that this is
  strange. Category: exec-male.
- Dilara Ozan, 41 — HR Business Partner, remote, appears by video call
  and email. Scrupulously, inflexibly fair, and the only person in the
  company who can require a human decision instead of a generated one.
  She is the player's real protection and the protection only works if
  the player does the paperwork, which makes "write it down" a genuine
  strategy rather than a chore. Category: exec-female.

THE BRANCH — SENIOR (3)
- Vicky Pardoe, 51 — Office Manager, twenty-four years. Has actually run
  the branch through four managers and is exhausted by it. Knows how the
  player got the job. Gatekeeper who becomes the most valuable ally in
  the building if the player treats her as a colleague instead of a
  resource, and the branch's quiet executioner if they do not. Second
  most important human in the Adventure. Category: floor-female.
- Aiden Fitch, 36 — Senior Sales. Applied for the player's job, was
  interviewed, was told he was a strong candidate. Charming, generous
  with his time, first to offer help, and documenting everything. Does
  not think of himself as the villain and is not one; he thinks he is
  correcting a mistake. Category: floor-male.
- Gail Tancred, 55 — Dispatch Supervisor. Runs the back of the unit like
  a ship and holds the sales floor in open contempt. Says exactly what
  she thinks in her own warehouse and nothing at all in a meeting. Signs
  the west shed transfers, has signed them for six years, and has a
  filing box of the paperwork she was never asked for. Also the
  unwilling custodian of a pallet of £40 paperclips.
  Category: warehouse-female.

SALES FLOOR (5)

TOKENS
---------
The engine matches NPCs with a plain case-sensitive substring scan, no
word boundaries. All fifteen tokens were checked programmatically at
authoring time against each other, against the thirteen location names,
and against a list of common capitalised words.

Two names were changed during authoring for collisions:
- Bev -> Gail. "Bev" fires inside "Beverage"/"Beverages", which are
  plausibly capitalised on a vending machine or a kitchen sign in this
  specific setting.
- MAXIM -> Clarence. The system's working name was MAXIM, which fires
  inside "Maximise" and "Maximum" — the two words this Adventure uses
  more than any other. It would have matched on nearly every line of
  head-office dialogue in the story. Clarence is a better name anyway,
  for the reasons in CLARENCE above.

Residuals, all judged non-risks in an office comedy: "Colm" inside
"Colmore", "Kev" inside "Kevlar", "Gail" inside "Gaily".

The janitor's store room is called THE CUPBOARD, not "Merv's Cupboard",
specifically so the location name does not carry his token and drag him
into every scene set there — the same trap Hollowburn avoided by naming
Jonah's room the Box Room.

STANDARD UNIFORMS (Stage A baseline portraits)
---------------------------------------------------
Eight categories, each combined with an NPC's identity line and a
generic neutral background at generation time — not written per-NPC, so
changing one line updates every NPC sharing that Category.

The garments are ordinary workwear; the cut and fit are written
flatteringly, matching the fan-service framing used in Solmere and
Halcyon. Every category except system carries "fully clothed, clearly an
adult".
- floor-male / floor-female: cheap branch-office business wear, CLIPCORP
  lanyard, sleeves pushed up, a little rumpled by the afternoon.
- warehouse-male / warehouse-female: branded hi-vis over work polo,
  steel-capped boots, ear defenders round the neck, box knife on the
  belt.
- exec-male / exec-female: a considerably better suit than anyone else's,
  worn with the confidence of someone who is leaving at four.
- maintenance-male: navy work trousers and a faded polo with the old
  Pemberton crest, keys on a belt loop, sleeves rolled over forearms.
  Merv only, and the only person in the building still wearing the old
  branding.
- system: no clothing and no body. A smiling cartoon paperclip with two
  large friendly eyes, drawn in flat 1997 clip-art style, floating in a
  small bordered window on a beige CRT screen. Clarence only, following
  the Marra's "entity" category in Hollowburn as the precedent for a
  single-member category.

No wetLocations and no poolUniforms — an office has no wet locations,
and the wet-outfit swap is guarded on both fields being present
(Code/Bottom Panel.txt line 4576), so omitting them is inert rather than
broken. clothingStates is retained at the Solmere/Meridian level.

playerCategoryTemplate: floor-{playerGender}. The player is always the
branch manager, so like Halcyon the template has a single variable.

TIME PHASES
--------------
An office day, not a calendar day, following Halcyon's shooting-day

day. Merv is already there. So is a welcome email, and the leaderboard

OPEN / NOT YET DECIDED
---------------------------
- Whether the branch survives is unfixed, and stays that way now the
  arc exists. The six endings are all about the player's ninety days;
  what happens to Unit 4 and to the west shed a year later is
  deliberately not written into canon, so the narration AI can land it
  from the state of the five problems. `In Writing` is explicitly
  instructed not to resolve it either.
- Whether Clarence should ever be shown to be wrong in a way it cannot
  paper over. Current position: no. It is never caught, never cornered,
  and never has a comeuppance, because the moment it loses an argument
  it becomes a character in a story about AI instead of the weather the
  story happens in. The counter-argument is that a comedy usually wants
  its antagonist punctured once.
- Whether Merv should have a reason for being the way he is. Current
  position: no. He is not hiding a dead wife or a lost son; he is a
  sixty-eight-year-old man who stopped performing for people about
  thirty years ago and finds it restful. Giving him a wound would make
  him a type. The counter-argument is that a late reveal would land hard
  precisely because he is the one character the player trusts.
- Clarence's data-quality exception on Merv resolves as the hidden
  `merv_indexed` milestone, and it is an ending: `An Unresolved Item`,
  fired deterministically the moment he lands on the leaderboard. It
  gates behind `the_number` so a day-three tidy-up cannot end a
  playthrough, and it is written to be played small — a record put
  right, a name added to a list.
- What DECOMMISSIONING refers to. Deliberately unresolved, with three
  named wrong answers in the lore.
- Whether Meeting Room 1 ever gets explained. Currently a pure running
  gag with no answer written.
- What actually happened to the four previous managers. Sketched, not
  fixed: one was promoted out, one had a breakdown, one was quietly
  removed and one simply stopped coming in. Which is which is left to
  play, Halcyon-style, because the Archive, Merv and Clarence can each
  supply an answer and it is better if they disagree.
- Whether the win98 skin's document pane should hold a serif or a sans.
  It is currently Times at 17px/1.65, which reads as a word-processor
  page and is the reason the skin stays legible over sixty turns. A sans
  would be more period-accurate to the chrome and worse to read.

LOCATION SCENERY (image prompts)
-----------------
Every location in the list above has a `locationScenery` entry in
Pemberton.json — the room, two or three fixed physical features and its light,
around 20 words. That text replaces the bare location name in the image
prompt's setting line, on inline, on-demand and composite images alike.
Keep entries short: the line appears in every prompt for that room, and a
composite repeats everything else once per character, so scenery is what
pushes a group prompt past the length this backend truncates at.
Image style for this Adventure: painted anime style, professional photo, front on shot
