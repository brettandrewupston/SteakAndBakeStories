THE OVERFLOW

The line runs three shifts. It has run three shifts since 2019, and the
output figure on the wall is completely true.

The orders have not tripled.

Nobody has ever said this out loud in a meeting, because the number on
the wall is green and going up and questioning it is the sort of thing
that gets noticed.

The clips go into the old west shed. It is not on the site plan anybody
uses, it does not appear in the stock system, and it has no cost code,
because Clarence classifies it as inventory in transit. It has been in
transit for six years. It is full to the roof and the doors have started
to be difficult to open.

Everyone half knows. Gail Tancred signs the transfers and keeps her own
copies. Kev Mullane drives them across and has stopped looking at what
he unloads. Tomasz Wrona has been further inside than anybody and
described it once, to one person, in the car park. Merv Hackett has a
key, because Merv has every key.

Nobody has assembled it into a sentence. It has never needed hiding,
because nobody has ever had both the authority to ask and no reason yet
not to — until the fifth manager in three years walked in on a Monday
morning with no history here at all.

There is nothing supernatural in the west shed. It is a building full of
paperclips, and everything about how it got that way is procedurally
correct, which is the frightening part.
