MARQUE — ADVENTURE CONTENT
===========================================================
Last updated: 30/08/2026

Eighth Adventure for the Story Generator. Architecture and engine decisions
live in the parent folder's Goals.txt and Features.txt — this file is this
Adventure's canon only: premise, world, rules, ports, and the parts of the
cast that are settled.

Adventure id: marque. Display title: "Marque". Files are named Marque.* to
match the id, consistent with Solmere / Meridian / Halcyon / Hollowburn /
Pemberton / Gallant / Vermillion.

A playable version exists and is registered in the ADVENTURES array:
`Marque.json`, a town file and a schematic map for each of the four ports,
the eight crew, one shipwright per port, ten sea encounters and a short arc.
What it does not yet have is depth — the ports are one person each, there is
no lore and no skin. This file stays the brief the rest is written from. See
OUTSTANDING at the end.

The engine holds one town at a time — see `Reference/Engine Design/Town
Files.md` — so the four ports are four town files merged onto a ship file
that carries the crew and everything else that travels. No Features.txt entry
blocks it any more; what remains is content.

PREMISE
----------
The Caribbean of the 1660s — the buccaneer era, not the later Golden Age.
Spain holds everything and is hated for it. The French have Tortuga, the
English have Port Royal, the Dutch have Curacao, and Santo Domingo is the
Spanish port on the same island the buccaneers hunt cattle on. Nobody
controls anything past the harbour mouth, and privateering commissions
keep most of what happens out there half-legal.

The player is a captain holding a commission taken out under false
pretence. They did not want a war, a cause or a fortune; they wanted a
legal reason to sail and to keep moving. The paper is the means, not the
plot.

The Adventure is deliberately loose. There is no single question the story
is asking and no conspiracy to unpick. It is a voyage, and what happens is
what a voyage turns up.

STRUCTURE
------------
A voyage rather than a place. A port, then a crossing, then another port,
with the choice of where to sail made on leaving. Each port carries its
own map and its own cast of roughly ten. The crew travel with the player
throughout and are the only constant.

That gives two classes of relationship, and they are authored differently:

- The crew compound across the whole story. They are written to last a
  hundred turns and are where depth goes.
- Port people are built, left behind, and returned to later. A port gives
  each of them roughly two scenes, so they are written sharp and
  single-note — one want, one angle, memorable fast.

Returning to a port with what was done there still standing is the payoff
the persisted relationship number is for, and it is something a
single-location Adventure cannot produce.

SAILING
----------
Leaving is a deliberate act with a Travel control beside Send, a destination
list, and a confirmation that says a course once set holds until the ship
makes port. It is enabled only while the captain is aboard.

Crossing length is `crossingTurns` on each town, so the map has real
distances in it: Santo Domingo is four beats away and sits on the same island
as Tortuga, Tortuga and Port Royal are six from anywhere, and Curacao is nine
because it is far south off the Main. For those beats no port is loaded at
all: the eight crew and the six shipboard locations are the entire world, and
the narrator is told the ship is on passage and how far is left. That stretch
is what the Sails upgrade shortens.

The distances are what make the route a decision. Curacao has the hulls and
costs the most sea to reach; Santo Domingo has the sails, is barely a voyage
away, and is the one port that would hang the captain on sight.

Ten sea encounters are authored, at most one to a passage, which puts a real
crossing between six and ten beats and leaves about one in five of them
quiet — a crossing where nothing happens is an outcome, not a gap. They are things the sea does — squalls, calms, a waterspout, whales,
water gone bad, a hand over the side, wreckage with someone still on it, a
sail astern that could be anything, a King's ship that will read the
commission properly, and once in a while something enormous under the hull
that every sailor aboard has heard described and none has seen.

PACING
---------
Milestone-driven, never a turn count, the same as every other Adventure
here. A port ends when its business is done — its shipwright's upgrade
bought, or the player simply chooses to sail. Pressure to move on comes
from heat rather than from a counter: the longer a captain stays, the more
likely the wrong people notice.

A full pass through all four ports plus crossings runs somewhere around a
hundred turns. AUTO_MEMORY_WINDOW keeps the last twenty raw, so by the
final port the first two exist as summaries. This is a fit rather than a
loss — the player did leave those people, and the relationship number is
what remembers them.

THE PORTS AND THE SHIPWRIGHTS
--------------------------------
Each port sells exactly one upgrade, and each upgrade works on its own
axis so the choice is a real one. All four are engine-side; only the cabin
reaches the prompt.

- Curacao (Dutch) — HULL. Fewer encounters. The Dutch built the best hulls
  in the world in this period and the fluyt is why they held the carrying
  trade.
- Santo Domingo (Spanish) — SAILS. Shorter crossings, so fewer beats at
  sea and fewer rolls, without touching the odds. It is also the upgrade
  that has to be bought inside a port that would hang the player, which is
  Santo Domingo's reason to exist beyond sitting on the map.
- Port Royal (English) — RUDDER. The choice to run. An encounter still
  rolls; the player is no longer stuck with it. It sits with the men who
  issue the commission, which is the right home for it.
- Tortuga (French) — CABIN. Tortuga was a plunder market and not a
  shipyard. A fine cabin is not commissioned there, it is furnished out of
  somebody else's ship. Implemented as a locationScenery swap.

The route therefore matters: wanting the hull means going to Curacao.

ENCOUNTERS
-------------
Authored as this Adventure's own content in its own file, rolled
engine-side. Pirates, krakens and sea monsters, weather, waterspouts.

Grounded, with one line holding it: these are things the SEA does.
Sailors in 1660 believed in krakens, so a kraken costs no cosmology.
Ghost ships and skeleton crews need one, and are parked rather than
written — see OPEN.

Only the encounter the roll returns ever reaches the prompt. The table
itself never does, so it can hold as many entries as are worth writing.

The intended weights on the roll are ship upgrades, the navigator's skill,
reputation in each nation's ports, cargo aboard, weather and the make-up
of the crew. None of those need to reach the prompt for the roll to use
them — the narrator never has to know why a kraken turned up.

Nothing in the encounter system drives combat the player must fight
through the engine. The story is free to let them win.

CAST
-------
Gender-mirrored through npcSets: a woman commanding a crew of men, or a
man commanding a crew of women. The ports themselves stay roughly even
either way.

The mirror follows Meridian's convention — one crew written twice, not two
crews. Same eight roles, same personalities and the same position in the
ship, with names and identity lines regendered between the two sets.

THE CREW (8)
Eight is a budget rather than a preference. The crew are aboard in every
scene and every port, so they are the permanent full-line load in the
prompt; eight of them alongside a port's ten is eighteen loaded at once,
which is the scale Pemberton already runs at.

Each role carries a hook rather than only a job:

- Quartermaster — the one who would have the ship if the captain fell.
  The standing counterweight, and the one who knows the commission is
  false (see below).
- Navigator — mechanically load-bearing: their skill is one of the weights
  on the encounter roll, which makes one relationship aboard worth actual
  money.
- Bosun — runs the deck, the work and the rota. The first to defend the
  captain to anyone who is not present.
- Gunner — wins the fights the ship should lose.
- Surgeon — the cost of those fights, in person.
- Carpenter — keeps the hull alive between shipwrights, and is therefore
  who says the upgrade is overdue.
- Cook — the ship's social centre and its memory. Knows what the crew
  actually think before the captain spends anything finding out.
- Topman — no rank, youngest aboard, and signed on for the same reason the
  captain did.

THE QUARTERMASTER KNOWS
The Quartermaster knows the commission was taken out under false pretence.

This is not a plot. There is nothing to uncover, no reveal, and no scene
where it comes out — it is simply true from the first turn, and both of
them know the other knows. What it buys is a permanent, speakable pressure
that costs no machinery: one person aboard could end the captain in any
port by saying so, and has not.

It sits beside the premise rather than driving it. The Adventure stays
loose.

Port casts are not yet written.

CONTENT
----------
High fan service, at Gallant's level in the portraits rather than below it.

The register is loose, minimal and hot-climate: sleeves gone, shirts open
or knotted, midriffs bare, legs bare, feet bare. What keeps it from
reading as costume is that the period supplies the reason for all of it —
tropical heat, no fresh water to spare for washing, and clothing cut down
from prizes to fit a body it was never made for. Nothing anachronistic is
named: a linen shirt with the sleeves hacked off is the tank top, sailcloth
slops cut short and belted low on the hips are the short shorts, a shirt
knotted under the chest is the crop top. Same amount of skin, and it reads
as the world instead of as a costume.

Both genders carry the same emphasis. The male categories are as exposed
and as worked as the female ones, not a modest counterpart to them.

Every category carries "fully clothed, clearly an adult", with
clothingStates handling everything past baseline. That pairing is the
guardrail and neither half is optional at any heat level. The standard
minor-safety layer sits underneath all of it, unchanged — see Safety
Layer.txt and Goals.txt SAFETY.

THE COMMISSION
-----------------
The commission can be lost — revoked, expired, or exposed as the false
thing it always was. Losing it is a turn in the middle of the story and
never an ending.

What it changes is the standing of every port. A captain with the paper is
a privateer that three of the four nations will trade with; a captain
without it is a pirate everywhere, including in the port that issued it.
The ports, the shipwrights and the encounter weights all stay exactly where
they were — what moves is the cost of walking into any of them.

THE ARC
----------
Three acts, ten milestones, five endings. The acts turn on milestones and
never on a turn count.

A loose Adventure cannot end on a plot, because there is no question it
has been asking. It ends on a choice instead — where the captain stops and
who with — and the four ports mean that choice is already built into the
map.

ACT I — THE PAPER HOLDS, until commission_lost. A privateer with a
commission three of the four nations will trade with. The act teaches the
loop: sail, arrive, buy the shipwright's work, leave.

ACT II — WITHOUT IT, until the_price. The same ports, the same
shipwrights and the same encounter table, all of it costing more. Santo
Domingo goes from risky to close to suicidal.

ACT III — WHERE YOU STOP. Every scene bends toward the answer.

MILESTONES
- first_crossing — the player sails from one port to another. The loop
  proves itself and the map stops being one place.
- first_upgrade — a shipwright's work bought and fitted. Establishes what
  a port is actually for.
- spoken — the thing the Quartermaster knows is said out loud between the
  two of them. Not a reveal, since both have known since the first turn:
  the milestone is that it stops being unsaid.
- weathered — the ship comes through an encounter that should have cost
  her. The table stops being scenery.
- commission_lost — the paper is gone: revoked, expired, or exposed.
  Closes Act I. No structural prerequisite, deliberately, and the world
  does this TO the captain rather than requiring them to earn it — the
  same reasoning as Pemberton's review_set and Gallant's the_offer. A
  cautious player who could never trigger it would never reach Act II, and
  an Adventure a passive player cannot finish is broken.
- all_four (needs first_crossing) — every port walked in.
- full_rig (needs all_four) — hull, sails, rudder and cabin, all bought.
  The completionist route, and the reason the map is worth the whole
  circuit.
- why_they_signed — a member of the crew says why they actually came. The
  crew's own beat, and the payoff for the depth they carry.
- the_price — a nation names what legitimacy would cost. Closes Act II. No
  prerequisite, for the same reason commission_lost has none.
- the_choice (needs the_price) — the captain answers.

ENDINGS
- Commissioned (the_choice:commission) — a real commission, from a real
  nation, at their price. The captain wanted the paper and now belongs to
  the people who issue it. Written as a job rather than a triumph.
- Ashore (the_choice:ashore) — the captain stops, in one of the four
  ports, with whoever they built. Which port it is IS the ending, and it
  reads differently in each.
- Still Sailing (the_choice:sailing) — every offer refused. The truest
  ending for someone who only ever wanted a reason to keep moving, and the
  one that costs the most to take.
- Taken — the assizes and the rope. Reachable at any point rather than
  gated behind the arc.
- The Ship — she is lost, or the crew are. The failure that is not a
  hanging, and reachable at any point.

No ending resolves the question of whether the commission was ever
legitimate, because the Adventure never asks it. It was false from the
first turn and everyone who mattered already knew.

OUTFITS AND LOCATIONS
------------------------
Declares privateQuarters for the captain's cabin — the parallel
location-triggered set logged in Features.txt, not wetLocations. It adds
wetLocations only if a port turns out to want a beach.

The wardrobe sits on the ship file rather than being split across the
towns, so one table keeps every portrait consistent. This is reversible:
uniforms are a lookup keyed by category and moving them later is a copy,
not a migration.

CREW BASELINE (crew-male / crew-female)
The eight aboard, and the register the whole Adventure is pitched from.
Each category is combined with an NPC's identity line and a neutral
background at generation time, so changing one line updates every NPC
sharing it.

- crew-female: a length of loose cloth tied across the front and knotted,
  leaving the shoulders, arms, back and midriff bare; short leather
  shorts cut high and belted low on the hips; bare legs; bare feet; a
  knife on a lanyard. Everything sun-bleached, salt-stiff and worn thin,
  and everything re-cut by hand to fit a body it was not made for.
- crew-male: a linen shirt with the sleeves torn away and the front open
  or gone entirely, bare chest and bare arms; short leather breeches cut
  above the knee and belted low; bare legs; bare feet; a knife on a
  lanyard. Same cloth, same salt, same hand-cut fit.

The leather is not a concession to the look — it is the most historically
literal thing in the file. Buccaneers were cattle hunters on Hispaniola
before they were pirates, and hide taken off the animals they killed is
what the word comes from. Leather in this heat is exactly what these
people would be wearing.

Both categories carry the same emphasis and the same amount of skin,
per CONTENT above, and both carry "fully clothed, clearly an adult".

Remaining categories are not yet decided — see OPEN.

FILE SPLIT
-------------
One authoritative ship file carrying everything Adventure-wide: crew, arc,
milestones, endings, relationship labels, time phases, clothing states,
image style, wardrobe, setup fields, prompt rules.

A thin file per town carrying only its locations, its map, its scenery,
its own NPCs and its lore URLs. Nothing Adventure-wide lives in a town
file, so a town can never contradict the arc.

A consequence worth having: the token collision sweep runs per town
against the crew, rather than globally across every name in the Adventure.

OPEN / NOT YET DECIDED
---------------------------
- Whether skeleton crews and ghost ships are ever real here. Parked, not
  rejected. It is a tone decision rather than a content one — it changes
  what the Spanish and the Navy are for, and every NPC written before it
  is settled could need writing again.
- Crew names, identity lines and personalities, and the regendered second
  set that mirrors them.
- The uniform categories past crew-male / crew-female: whether the eight
  aboard split into officer and crew tiers, what the captain wears (the
  player's own category, so playerCategoryTemplate depends on it), and
  whether each port carries its own shore pair or the four share one.
- The privateQuarters set itself, which CONTENT's level applies to before
  clothingStates does.
- Relationship labels, time phases, setup fields and image settings.

OUTSTANDING
--------------
1. Everything the Adventure needs exists. Needs a push.
2. Blocked on one Features.txt entry: random encounters.
3. The maps are schematic placeholders — flat labelled boxes carrying the
   right `loc-` ids, not drawn maps.
4. All four port casts are at ten. Token sweep clean across both crew
   rosters against every town, no schedule naming a location its town does
   not declare, and every category covered by the wardrobe.
5. Lore is twenty files: four topics and eight crew on the ship file, and a
   place entry and a cast entry on each town file.
6. The encounter table is sea-only. Nothing fires in port.
7. Untested. No Begin has been run against Marque.
