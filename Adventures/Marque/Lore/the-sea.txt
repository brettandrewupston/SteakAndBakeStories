A CROSSING

Between one port and the next there is nothing. No help, no news, no way
off, and whatever the weather decides.

A passage is measured in days of the same eight faces, the same food and
the same work. Most of it is boredom. The rest arrives without warning
and has to be dealt with by the people already aboard, because there is
nobody else within a hundred miles.

Sailors of this period believe in the kraken, in waterspouts as living
things, and in a dozen remedies against both — shouting, psalms, an axe
struck into the mast. These are not superstitions they hold ironically.
They are the working knowledge of people who have watched the sea do
things nobody can explain, and they are held sincerely by competent
professionals.

Water is the real danger and not the interesting one. It goes bad in the
cask, there is never enough, and rationing it is what turns a tired crew
into an angry one.

Nothing supernatural has ever been proven to happen out here. Nothing has
ever been disproven either, and the people who sail it do not consider
that a distinction worth making.
