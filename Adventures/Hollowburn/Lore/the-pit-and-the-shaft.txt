THE PIT AND THE SHAFT

Hollowburn Colliery sank its first shaft in 1871 and its workings ran
east, out under the North Sea, for miles. This is not unusual for this
coast and every family in the village knows it. Men walked to work
under the water and came up under it.

1947. A fall in the east district. Eleven men. Ten were brought up.
The eleventh was never recovered and the district was sealed with him
in it, because that was cheaper and because the sea was coming
through. There is a memorial in St Aidan's with eleven names on it and
the eleventh has been rubbed at by so many thumbs over eighty years
that it can no longer be read.

That name is the single most important object in the story. Sheila
Rudd's ledger has it. Denny Aitchison can find it on the plans. Nell
Moore is standing next to it every Sunday and has never once looked.

1968. The colliery closed the upcast shaft at the top end of the
village and capped it with rubble and a signed promise. Denny was on
that team. It was done cheaply, it was done fast, and it was not done
properly. The estate was built over the top of it in the seventies.

The cul-de-sac sits on the cap. The Blythe house is the last one on
the cul-de-sac.

1989. Duncan Fell, seventeen, of number eleven, stopped sleeping,
told nobody, and left the village. There is a gap in the Blythe
family photographs on the landing where somebody used to be, and
nobody in that house has ever remarked on it, including the people who
walk past it forty times a day.

Evelyn Fell was fifteen that year. She is Evelyn Blythe now and she
still lives four doors from where it happened.
