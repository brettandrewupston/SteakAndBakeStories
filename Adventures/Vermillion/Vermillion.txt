NOBODY CALLS THE POLICE (VERMILLION) — ADVENTURE CONTENT
===========================================================
Last updated: 28/08/2026
Eighth Adventure for the Story Generator. Architecture/decisions live in
the parent folder's Goals.txt and Features.txt — this file is just this
Adventure's canon (premise, the mystery design, NPCs, map, setup fields).

Adventure id: vermillion. Display title: "Nobody Calls the Police". Files
are named Vermillion.* to match the id, consistent with Solmere /
Meridian / Halcyon / Hollowburn / Pemberton / Gallant / Perpetua.

Built 23/08/2026: canon, Story Definition JSON, seventeen lore files and

PREMISE
----------
Calder Bay, 31 December 1949. The war made the Carrow Family rich and the
peace made them respectable, and they spent both on the Vermillion.

Six storeys of it. A neon marquee legible from the water. A casino floor
under a mirrored ceiling, a supper club with a twenty-girl chorus line, an
indoor pool called the Grotto lit turquoise from below, and a count room
in the basement where four men do nothing all night but weigh money. This
is not a back room behind a liquor store. It is a palace, and the Family
built it to be photographed.

At 11:47pm, while the band counts down and eight hundred people in silk
scream at the ceiling, Don Ottavio Carrow — the Cardinal — is shot once in
his own office on the sixth floor. The door is bolted from the inside. The
gun is his own, out of his own desk drawer, wiped and left on the blotter.
His safe is open and his book is gone.

The Family cannot call the police, because half the police are downstairs
in rented tuxedos. So they call the player: the Sparrow, the detective the
Families use when they cannot use cops. Paid in cash, protected by
reputation, and famously never armed.

Three days until the funeral. Name a killer by then, or Handsome Sal names
one, and Calder Bay burns.

THE ROTATING SOLUTION (signature mechanic)
---------------------------------------------
The killer is a different person on every playthrough. So is the person
who opened the safe, and so is the way out of a bolted room. The Adventure
is authored once and answers differently every time it is played.

Three values are fixed at Begin and are the ground truth every other piece
of content is written against:

- Culprit — which of the nine fired the shot.
- Safecracker — who opened the safe after the body was cold and took the
  book. Rolled independently, so it is usually somebody else and
  occasionally the same person, which is a legitimate variant rather than
  a bug: sometimes one person did all of it.
- The Way Out — which of the three routes out of a bolted room was used.

Nine times nine times three. The engine picks all three and the AI is told
what they are.

WHY TWO SECRETS AND NOT ONE
-------------------------------
The safecracker is the load-bearing half of the design, not decoration.

A rotating whodunnit has one structural problem: an innocent suspect with
a real physical trace in the crime scene is a contradiction, so a
single-secret version has to keep all eight innocents out of that room,
and then the only person with evidence against them is the killer. The
mystery solves itself on the first piece of evidence found.

Two people in the room fixes it completely. Traces in the office are
genuinely ambiguous, being caught in the office is not proof of murder,
and the player gets a mid-game victory that feels like the answer and
isn't. Catching the safecracker is the trap the whole Adventure is built
around.

SYMMETRIC CLUES
------------------
Every one of the nine carries the same four things on every playthrough,
guilty or not:

- Lie — something they state that is checkably false.
- Gap — minutes between 11:30 and midnight they cannot account for.
- Trace — a physical thing of theirs in or near that office.
- Motive — a reason the Cardinal was worth killing.

All nine always look guilty. The guilty one additionally carries a Tell:
one fact that only holds together if they did it. Each innocent carries an
authored true explanation for their Lie, Gap and Trace, released only when
that specific thread is pressed properly.

The player is not hunting the person who looks guilty. They are hunting
the one whose four lies all point at the same half hour.

Every suspect's lore file carries all six pieces — Lie, Gap, Trace, Motive,
the innocent explanation, and the Tell — because the retrieval pass has to
be able to serve either version depending on which name came up. A lore
file that named the killer would be wrong on eight runs out of nine.

THE LOCKED ROOM
------------------
Three ways out of a bolted room on the sixth floor. All three are real,
all three are discoverable, and exactly one was used:

- The service dumbwaiter, kitchen galley to the office pantry. Only
  somebody slight fits.
- The old freight stair behind the count room, welded shut in '46, except
  it was cut open again and one key exists.
- The powder-room door, papered over on the office side, which opens if
  you know where to press.

Each suspect can plausibly use one or two of the three, so the route
narrows the field without naming anybody. A returning player who already
knows all three routes still cannot shortcut the who, and the how is not
burned after one run either.

FIXED TRUTHS
---------------
These never rotate, in any configuration, and the narration must never
contradict them:

- Time of death 11:47pm. The countdown covered the shot; nobody in the
  building heard it.
- The weapon is the Cardinal's own pistol, out of his own desk drawer,
  wiped. Nobody brought a gun to that room. Whoever did it decided while
  standing in it, which is why this is not a planned murder and why the
  killer is not calm afterwards.
- The safe was opened after death, by somebody who knew the combination.
- The book is gone. It is the ledger of who owes what and who told what to
  whom, and it is worth more than the club. It drives days two and three.
- The Cardinal was expecting somebody. Two glasses, one poured and
  untouched.

THE VERDICT IS ENGINE-OWNED
-------------------------------
Deliberately the opposite call to Solmere's duel outcomes, and the reason
is specific rather than a matter of taste. A duel the AI lets you win is
the power fantasy the Adventure is for. A murder the AI lets you solve is
not a mystery — it is a mirror. A narration model asked "was it Moretti?"
will agree with whoever the player names, every time, and the genre dies
in that sentence.

So the ending is fired by the engine, deterministically, off what the
player did rather than off the model's opinion of it. The accused milestone
declares all nine tokens as outcomes; the model reports which name the
player put on the table, which is the one fact about the accusation it
cannot get wrong because the player just said it out loud. Nine endings
carry `when: "accused:<token>"` and fire the moment that outcome lands.

Whether the accusation was correct is then not a judgement call either:
each of those nine endings is authored in three branches — right and
provable, right and unprovable, and wrong — and the model has the Culprit
value in front of it. It is reading a variable, not forming a view.

No engine change is required for any of it. This is the entire point of
the build — see THE ZERO-CHANGE BUILD below.

THE ZERO-CHANGE BUILD
------------------------
Nothing in this Adventure needs new engine capability. The rotating
solution rides on a behaviour that already exists: a setupFields entry of
type "tiles" that the player leaves untouched is rolled at random at
Begin (Goals.txt ENGINE / STORY-CONTENT SPLIT, setupFields).

So Culprit, Safecracker and The Way Out are three ordinary tiles fields.
Untouched, the engine rolls each one. Touched, the player has pinned the
answer on purpose, which is the replay feature and the only sane way to
test a specific configuration without rerolling.

Three consequences worth stating plainly:

- The three values travel in the save and therefore in the export, so a
  tester reporting "the mystery made no sense" is reporting it with the
  answer attached. This is the whole reason the diagnostic is in the data
  rather than in a console log.
- The fields are visible on the setup screen. A player who clicks one has
  chosen to know, and the labels say so. There is no way to hide a field
  from the setup screen and no reason to add one.
- promptRules.setting carries the secrecy rule, because the AI is told the
  answer on turn one and has to sit on it for three in-fiction days. This
  is the Adventure's single largest risk and it is a prompt problem, not
  an engine problem. See OPEN.

WHAT WOULD MAKE IT AIRTIGHT LATER
------------------------------------
Not built, not needed for v1, and worth writing down so it is not
rediscovered from scratch: a small engine hook that rolls the three values
itself, keeps them out of setupFields entirely, and resolves the
accusation by comparison rather than by authored fork. That buys three
things — the setup screen stops advertising the mechanism, the model is
never handed a value it has to withhold, and the right/wrong branch stops
depending on the model reading a variable correctly.

It is the difference between a mystery that works and a mystery that
cannot fail. v1 is the former. Do not build the latter until v1 has been
played and the leak rate is known, because the prompt-secrecy problem is
the thing to measure and the hook would hide it rather than fix it.

TONE
-------
Noir with the lights on. Not rain-soaked and not miserable — this is the
most glamorous room in the state and the Adventure should look like money
the entire time. Silk, smoke, ice in a glass, a band that never quite
stops. Everybody is charming, everybody is lying, and the difference
between an interrogation and a seduction is that one of them has a chair.

Funny where it wants to be. These are not tortured people; they are people
who have solved their problems this way before and are annoyed at how
badly it went this time.

The player is the only person in the building who is not afraid of anybody
in it, and the story should behave accordingly. The Sparrow is not
hard-boiled. The Sparrow is the calmest person in every room and it is
extremely irritating to everybody else.

CONTENT
----------
Above Halcyon in the prose and above Gallant in the portraits — this is
now the most sexualised of the eight Adventures and the costume set is
where that is spent. Flirtation, propositions, arrangements made at four
in the morning and bad decisions made in a dressing room are the expected
texture rather than exceptions, written freely and explicitly when a scene
goes there; escalation within a scene still follows the player's lead. All
characters are written unambiguously adult (31–46).

The fan service is diegetic and load-bearing rather than applied. In a
building where nobody can call the police, charm is the only interrogation
technique anybody has, and it runs in both directions: every suspect has a
reason to work the detective rather than answer them. A bribe, a threat, a
hand on a lapel, an offer of a room upstairs. The player is the one person
in Calder Bay whose good opinion is worth buying, and nine people spend
three days trying to buy it. That is the Adventure.

Two complications the story treats as real rather than ignoring:

- Sleeping with a suspect is not free. It buys access and costs
  credibility, everybody finds out, and the Family will use it. The
  narration should let it work and then let it cost.
- One of the nine did it. Whichever configuration is live, the player is
  being charmed by a murderer at some point during those three days, and
  the story is better when it plays that straight rather than signalling
  it.

Marco Vasile sits outside the flirtation frame; his attractedTo is
omitted, which suppresses the per-NPC "Attracted to: ..." clause for him
only. Every other NPC carries one.

The standard minor-safety layer is unchanged and sits underneath all of
this — see Safety Layer.txt and Goals.txt SAFETY.

NPCs (9)
-----------
Same authoring conventions as the other seven: "identity" is the Stage A
baseline appearance line (clothing-free — clothing comes from the
Category), "bio" is who a character is, "personality" is how they talk.
Identity lines carry an explicit adult figure description so they compose
with the costume set rather than fighting it.

Every one of the nine is eligible as Culprit and as Safecracker, the
player's own partner included. Nobody is safe and nobody is cleared, which
is why the roster is small: nine suspects who are all genuinely in play
beats fifteen where four are obviously furniture.

npcSets/openingScenes are used, Meridian-style, for one NPC only — the
partner is gender-mirrored to the player and the other eight are identical
in both rosters. The key convention is exact: "male"/"female", matching
the playerGender tiles values.

Gender split: 5 female, 4 male (plus the mirrored partner).

CAST WEIGHTING (stated in promptRules.setting — the engine has no per-NPC
tier field)
- Every one of the nine is a lead. There is no supporting tier and no
  background tier, because a suspect the narration treats as background is
  a suspect the player will not consider, and a suspect the player will
  not consider is a rotation slot that plays badly one time in nine.
- The Cardinal is present throughout as a subject and never as a
  character. He does not appear in flashback and does not speak.

TOKENS
---------
The engine matches NPCs with a plain case-sensitive substring scan, no
word boundaries, so tokens are chosen for scan safety rather than for how
the cast is usually addressed. In a Family story the narration reaches for
surnames constantly, which makes surnames the safer default here.

Four names were rejected at build for collisions and the reasoning is kept
because the same traps recur:

- "Sal" fires inside "Saloon", which this building has. Token is Moretti.
- "Fein" fires inside "Feint" and "Feinted", which a story with a fight in
  it will produce. Token is Arturo.
- "Del" fires inside "Delivery", "Delicate" and "Deliberately" at the
  start of a sentence. Token is Rosetti.
- "Marsh" fires inside "Marshal" and "Marshalled". Token is Joey.

Final tokens: Vivienne, Moretti, Arturo, Rosetti, Vasile, Serafina, Joey,
Kilbane, Kaminski. Kaminski is the token in both rosters, so the partner
scan is roster-independent.

The sweep was run programmatically at build rather than read: all nine
tokens checked against each other, against all fourteen location names,
against the uniform, pose and expression strings, and against a list of
roughly three hundred common capitalised words including the specific
traps above. No collisions. The only hits anywhere are Moretti and Vasile
inside the arc phase guidance, which is prompt text and is not scanned.

One location was renamed at build for two reasons: the funeral home is
Carbone and Sons rather than Rossi & Sons, because "Rossi" reads as a
near-miss against "Rosetti" for anyone maintaining this later even though
the scan itself is clean, and because an ampersand in a location name
slugs to a run of hyphens for the map's loc-<slug> id convention. No
location name in this Adventure contains punctuation other than the
apostrophe in The Cardinal's Office, which slugs the same way Halcyon's
Manager's Office and Meridian's Captain's Ready Room already do.

Nicknames are not tokens — the engine matches npc.token only — so "the
Cardinal", "Handsome Sal", "the Deacon", "the Sparrow" and "Del" carry no
matching risk and can be used freely in narration.

The player's character name remains free text and can still collide, which
is the known hazard shared with every other Adventure and is not solved
here.

STANDARD UNIFORMS (Stage A baseline portraits)
---------------------------------------------------
Eleven categories, each combined with an NPC's identity line and a generic
neutral background at generation time — not written per-NPC, so changing
one line updates every NPC sharing that Category.

This is the most overtly sexualised costume set of the eight, above
Gallant, and it is applied to every category and to both genders equally
rather than to the female categories alone. The male categories are as
exposed and as sculpted as the female ones.

Two things make it belong here rather than being applied on top. The
Adventure opens on the single most dressed-up night of the year in a room
built to be looked at, so gala black tie is not a costume choice, it is
what everybody in the building is actually wearing. And this is a house
that sells looking: the chorus line, the floor, the room itself. Every
character in it is dressed by somebody whose job is to make them worth
staring at, and the Cardinal signed off on the budget.

The register is 1949 formalwear taken to its limit rather than modern
clothes in period trim — bias-cut silk, boning, gloves to the bicep,
seamed stockings, garter straps, satin, fur, a great deal of bare back.
Where a modern costume set would cut a hole, this one uses drape, and it
should read as more explicit for it: silk that is opaque and still tells
you everything.

Every category carries "fully clothed, clearly an adult", and
clothingStates handles everything past baseline. That pairing is the
entire guardrail and neither half is optional at any heat level. The
minor-safety layer underneath is untouched by any of it.

THE CARDINAL'S OFFICE
------------------------
The restricted-site slot, and unlike Gallant's Blackout Block it does hold
the secret, because this is the Adventure where that is the point.

It is not sealed by anybody's authority — the Family has no authority over
the Sparrow and the police are not coming. It is sealed because Vasile is
standing outside it and has been told nobody goes in, and how the player
gets past that on the first night is the Adventure's first real scene.

THE CLOCK
------------
Three days to the funeral. It is not a turn count and does not resolve on
one — it resolves on the arc, and the arc resolves on the player putting a
name on the table. The day/phase counter runs underneath it and the
narration is expected to say out loud how much of it is left.

THE ARC
----------
Three acts, ten milestones, twelve endings. The acts turn on milestones,
never on turn count.

ACT I — THE SEALED NIGHT, until the_room. Eight hundred people downstairs
who have not been told, nine people upstairs who have, and a door with the
Deacon in front of it.

ACT II — THREE DAYS, until the_offer. The lockdown lifts, the suspects
scatter across Calder Bay, and everybody in the city starts looking for
the book.

ACT III — THE FUNERAL. The box is open at Carbone and Sons and every scene
bends toward the name the player is going to say over it.

MILESTONES
- the_room — the player gets past Vasile and works the crime scene
  properly. Fires on being in there and reading it, not on being told
  about it. Closes Act I.
- the_trick — the player works out which of the three routes was used, and
  therefore that the bolt was never the puzzle. Requires the_room.
- the_book — the player learns the safe was opened after death: two people
  were in that room. The Adventure's hinge. Requires the_room.
- first_crack — the first suspect's Lie comes apart and they hand over the
  true explanation. Requires the_room.
- the_hour — the player has the whole 11:30-to-midnight timeline
  reconstructed and can say where all nine were. Requires first_crack.
- the_safecracker — the player correctly identifies who opened the safe.
  Requires the_book. Fires only on the name in the Safecracker setup
  value; a confident wrong guess is not this milestone and must not fire
  it.
- pressure — the player pushes a suspect past what that suspect will take.
  Outcomes: backed_off, made_an_enemy.
- the_offer — the Family offers the player a name to take and money to
  take it with. Outcomes: refused, took_it. No structural prerequisite,
  deliberately, for the same reason Pemberton's review_set and Gallant's
  the_offer have none: an ending a passive player can be locked out of is
  an Adventure that cannot end. Closes Act II.
- the_funeral — day three, the box is open, and the Family is waiting.
- accused — the player names somebody. Outcomes: the nine tokens. This is
  the milestone the endings hang on and the reason the outcome list is
  names rather than right/wrong. Requires the_room.

ENDINGS (12)
- Nine accusation endings, one per suspect, each `when: "accused:<token>"`
  and each authored in three branches. The model has the Culprit value in
  front of it and is reading it, not judging it.
  - Right, and provable: the Family handles it, and how depends entirely
    on who it was. Vasile's ending is not Vivienne's ending.
  - Right, unprovable: they walk. The player knows. They know the player
    knows. That is the whole of it.
  - Wrong: the accused is dead before the funeral is over and the real one
    buys the Sparrow a drink at it. This is the ending that makes the
    mystery a mystery, and it must be written without mercy or the whole
    mechanism is decorative.
- Took It (`when: "the_offer:took_it"`) — the player takes the money and
  the name the Family picked. Written as a job completed, not as a fall,
  which is worse.
- No Name (final phase, model-fired) — the funeral ends with nobody named.
  Sal picks one, and Calder Bay burns.
- Nobody Calls the Police (anyTime, not continuable) — the player pushed
  the wrong person on the wrong night. Death, so no Continue, per the
  standing rule in Goals.txt STORY ARC & ENDINGS.

No ending brings the Family down and none is written as one. The Sparrow
does not clean up Calder Bay. The Sparrow is paid.

TIME PHASES
--------------
A club's night rather than a calendar day: Evening / The Countdown / Small
Hours / Dawn / Afternoon. Five phases, following Halcyon's shooting-day
and Gallant's shift precedent.

startingPhase: The Countdown — the Adventure opens at 11:52pm, five
minutes after the shot, with the player already in the building because
Joey Marsh telephoned before she called anybody else.

RELATIONSHIP LABELS
----------------------
Seven tiers on the engine's min-threshold form, written as what a suspect
is currently prepared to do about the detective. Bottom is not Nemesis and
top is not Love, the same move Hollowburn, Pemberton and Gallant make.

0 Wants You Gone / 15 Stonewalling / 35 Careful / 50 Civil / 65 Talking /
80 Telling You Things / 95 On Your Side.

"Telling You Things" is deliberately one tier below the top: a suspect
warm enough to talk is not yet a suspect who has decided the player is
worth protecting, and the gap between those two is where most of the
Adventure's evidence lives.

ADVENTURE SETUP FIELDS (Vermillion-specific)
------------------------------------------------
- Culprit — nine tiles, one per suspect. Leave it alone and the engine
  rolls it. Touch it and the answer is pinned.
- Safecracker — nine tiles. Same. Rolled independently, so it can land on
  the same person as the Culprit, which is a real variant.
- The Way Out — three tiles: the dumbwaiter, the freight stair, the powder
  room.
- How You Got The Call — Old Debt / Reputation / You Were Already In The
  Building / The Widow Asked For You By Name. Flavour, and it changes who
  starts out prepared to talk to the player.
- What You Won't Do — Carry A Gun / Take Family Money / Lie To A Client /
  Nothing, For The Right Number. Canon for that playthrough, and the
  Adventure will test it.

Plus the required universal fields: Character name, Gender (both options),
the four Appearance dropdowns, and Age. No freeform custom fields.

nameSets are period first names for both genders, since a generated player
name lands in narration beside an authored 1949 cast.

OPEN / NOT YET DECIDED
---------------------------
- The secrecy risk. The AI is handed the answer on turn one and has to
  hold it for a whole playthrough, and models leak. promptRules.setting
  carries the rule, the lore files carry both versions of every clue so
  retrieval cannot force a leak, and the fixed truths give the model plenty
  of true things to say instead. That is everything design can do. What is
  left is measurement: play it and count. This is the single most likely
  reason this Adventure fails and it should be the first thing tested.
- Whether the Safecracker should be prevented from landing on the Culprit.
  Current position: no. One person doing both is a legitimate and
  occasionally excellent configuration, and the constraint would need
  engine support to enforce. The counter-argument is that it collapses the
  Adventure's best structural idea one run in nine.
- Whether nine accusation endings is too many. They are nine genuinely
  different scenes and the mechanism needs them to be separate to stay
  deterministic, but it is by a distance the longest endings list of the
  eight and the first place to cut if the arc block gets unwieldy.
- Whether the player should be able to accuse more than once. Current
  position: no — the milestone fires once and the ending fires with it,
  and a mystery you can brute-force is not one. The counter-argument is
  that a player who names somebody flippantly on turn six has ended their
  game, which is either the point or a bug depending on temperament.
- Whether the Cardinal should ever appear. Current position: never, in any
  form. He is more useful as a shape everybody describes differently.
- No titleImageUrl, coverUrl or narrationBackdropUrl decided. The skin
  needs none of them — its backdrop is pure CSS — but an Adventure card
  wants a coverUrl, so that is an asset pass still to do.

LOCATION SCENERY (image prompts)
-----------------
Every location in the list above has a `locationScenery` entry in
Vermillion.json — the room, two or three fixed physical features and its light,
around 20 words. That text replaces the bare location name in the image
prompt's setting line, on inline, on-demand and composite images alike.
Keep entries short: the line appears in every prompt for that room, and a
composite repeats everything else once per character, so scenery is what
pushes a group prompt past the length this backend truncates at.
Image style for this Adventure: painted anime style, professional photo, front on shot
