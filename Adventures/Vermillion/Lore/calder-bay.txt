CALDER BAY

A working port city of four hundred thousand on a cold northern coast, rich
in a way it did not expect to be. Four years of war contracts turned the
waterfront into money, and the men who controlled the waterfront in 1942
control considerably more than that now.

Two Families. The Carrows took the docks, the freight, the unions and the
liquor licences. The Bellandis kept the produce markets, the numbers, the
south side and a long grudge. In 1946 the Carrows took the waterfront
outright. Eight months later two Bellandi uncles went into the bay off the
Ninth Street pier and nobody was charged, because the detective assigned to
it is at the Vermillion tonight in a rented tuxedo.

The truce is six weeks old and everybody expects it to end. Nobody expected
it to end like this.

The city itself is beautiful in a hard-edged way: neon on wet streets, deco
towers built on wartime money, a cathedral, an art museum the Cardinal paid
for, and a police department that is not so much corrupt as employed. The
newspapers know all of it and print none of it. Everyone in Calder Bay
understands the arrangement perfectly and would not describe it as an
arrangement.

Places worth knowing: the Adelphi Hotel bar, where federal men drink because
they think nobody looks. Carbone and Sons on Delancey, which buries both
Families and keeps them in separate rooms. The Ambrosia Baths, where the
Carrows talk business because you cannot wear a wire in a towel. The Ninth
Street pier.
