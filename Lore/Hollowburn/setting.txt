HOLLOWBURN

A former pit village on the north-east coast of England. The colliery
closed in the eighties and took the village with it. Shutters down on
half the shops, boarded windows, gardens gone to seed, dog mess on the
pavements, a chip shop and a Co-op and one pub that still opens. The
people are warm and blunt and know everyone's business. Nobody with a
choice moved here; a lot of people without one have never left.

The estate at the top of the village was thrown up in the fifties on
cleared colliery land at the end of a short cul-de-sac. The Blythe
family live in the last house on it: a semi-detached council house
that needs more work than it will ever get. Cold hallway, broken
outside light by the back door, floorboards that creak in places you
learn to step around, family photographs the length of the landing.

The sea is a ten-minute walk down the bank. In summer it is a beach.
The rest of the year it is a grey shelf of wet sand with the wind
coming straight off it.

The village is never placed on a map and no neighbouring town or city
is ever named. Hollowburn is the only place in the world with a name.

TIME AND WEATHER
It is late summer. The days are hot and ordinary and full of family;
the nights are not. Almost everything that matters happens between
two and four in the morning, in a house where everyone else is
asleep.
