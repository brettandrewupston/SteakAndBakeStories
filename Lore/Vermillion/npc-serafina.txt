SERAFINA BELLANDI

Heir to the Bellandi Family, 33, at the Vermillion under a six-week-old
truce, in white, alone, and staying. Cleverer than her father and
considerably harder than she looks. Her uncles Nico and Sandro went into
the bay off the Ninth Street pier in 1947 and the detective assigned to it
is downstairs in a rented tuxedo.

THE LIE
She says she never left the supper club floor. Two people put her on the
mezzanine, and the mezzanine is where the lifts are.

THE GAP
11:39 to 11:51.

THE TRACE
White fur fibres. In the mezzanine powder room, on the carpet of the
sixth-floor landing, and — this is the part that matters — caught in
the papered-over edge of the powder-room door on the office side.

THE MOTIVE
The waterfront, the uncles, and a war that pays. If the Carrows cannot
answer for their own Don inside their own building, the truce is worth
nothing by the weekend and the Bellandis take back the docks by March.

IF THEY ARE INNOCENT — the true explanation
She spent those twelve minutes counting. She was mapping the building for
her father — exits, stairs, sightlines, which doors are watched and
which are not — because she may not get inside it again for years and
she is not going to waste the only night she has. She went up through the
powder room because it connects, she found the panel by pressing every
panel in the room in turn, she looked at the papered-over door, and she
did not open it. Admitting it means admitting she broke the truce herself
on the night the Carrows lost their Don, in a building full of Carrows,
which is not survivable. She will admit it to exactly one kind of person:
someone who has already worked it out and offers her something for it.

IF THEY ARE THE CULPRIT — the tell
She knows the powder-room door opens. Not that it exists — that it
opens, and which way, and that you press at hip height. She could not have
learned that from any Carrow, and she says something that only makes sense
if she has been through it and come back.
